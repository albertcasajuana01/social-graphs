Week 07 · From meaning to measurement

Can the text rebuild the network?

A hyperlink is an editor's decision. A name is just words on the page. We found every character named in 29,006 sentences of the Marvel pages, built the network from names alone, and compared it with the hyperlinks pair by pair. Wherever the two disagreed, we read the wiki source to find out why.

01 · The questionIs a link just a name?

Last week we wrote that "a hyperlink from page A to page B means A mentions B by name". We built that post on the assumption and never checked it. This is the last week of the language block, so we tested it on the whole corpus:

If we deleted every hyperlink, could the words alone rebuild the Marvel network? And where the words and the links disagree, which one is wrong?

From names alone you can build two networks. The mention network has an edge A → B when A's page names B. It has the same shape as a link, so the two can be compared edge by edge. The co-mention network has an edge A – B when one sentence, on any of the 303 pages, names both. That edge is about who appears together, which a link doesn't record. In both, the hard part is deciding who a name refers to.

02 · MeasurementFinding the names is the hard part

Spider-Man's page calls him Spider-Man, Peter Parker, Peter and Parker. The other pages call 303 characters by about 600 different names, and many of those names are traps. We collected 835 candidate names from three places: the page titles, the characters' Wikidata aliases, and the parenthesis in each page's first sentence ("The Abomination (Emil Blonsky) is…"). Then we resolved them with one rule we can defend:

A name counts for a character only if Wikipedia itself sends that name to the character's page.

We asked Wikipedia where each of the 835 names leads, following redirects, and saved the answers. The rule settles most cases with no judgement on our part:

Three more rules handle what Wikipedia can't. Titles aren't characters: The Amazing Spider-Man #129, Spider-Man: No Way Home and Power Man and Iron Fist #73 are citations, so a name followed by an issue number, a colon or a sequel number doesn't count. We also drop the In other media sections, which are mostly film titles and cast lists. Some names are ordinary words: a word like force or storm doesn't count when it's glued to another capitalized word (Air Force, Sue Storm, Ebony Blade). And on its own page, a character's own names mean that character: on Johnny Blaze's page, "Ghost Rider" is Johnny Blaze. The exception is the 44 pages about a codename itself ("Bucky is the name used by several different characters…"), where the codename can mean any of its holders.

Then we read the output. We took 80 random name matches on other characters' pages and checked each one in its sentence. 72 were right (90%). The 8 misses were titles (Citizen V and the V-Battalion, Matt Fraction's Hawkeye series), other characters with the same name (Red Hulk, a 1970s Black Cat "not to be confused with the Spider-Man supporting character") and two codenames sent to the wrong holder. The full list is in the table at the end. We tuned the rules on two earlier samples and drew this one, with a new seed, only once the rules were frozen.

03 · The answerMostly yes, and then there's Hawkeye

Of the 1,784 links, 1,454 (81.5%) point to a character whom the linking page also names in its prose. In the other direction, 78% of the 1,869 pairs where a page names a character are also links. The text gets most of the way back to the network. So we compared, character by character, how many pages name them with how many link to them:

A name page is a page about a codename several characters have used ("Hawkeye is the name of several fictional characters…"). Names are resolved as in section 2. In other media sections are left out.

Hawkeye is the fifth most-named character in the corpus. Forty-three pages name him, 137 times, and not one links to his page. The corpus's Hawkeye page is a page about the name, which Clint Barton, Kate Bishop and others have all used. When an editor links the word Hawkeye, they link the person: 36 of our pages link "Hawkeye (Clint Barton)", 63 times in total. That article isn't in Wikipedia's Marvel Comics superheroes category, so it isn't one of our 303 nodes. In the week-1 network Hawkeye has in-degree zero, and every centrality we computed for him in weeks 1–3 was measured on a page nobody links to.

The top of Figure 1 is all name pages: Captain Marvel, Spider-Woman, Ms. Marvel, Ghost Rider, Black Knight, Bucky. Switch to Linked more than named and you get the people behind those names: the android Human Torch, Spider-Gwen, Jessica Drew, Mayday Parker. Text and links don't disagree at random. The text names roles, and the links pick people.

04 · Every disagreement, explainedWhy a link has no name, and a name has no link

To see why each pair disagrees, we needed the wiki source, not the rendered text. The source shows every [[target|shown text]] and where on the page it sits. We downloaded the last revision of each page on or before the course snapshot (26 August 2026). The links in those sources reproduce 1,781 of the course's 1,784 edges, so the course network is exactly the [[links]] in the source. Then we gave every disagreeing pair a reason:

Figure 2 · The two ledgers

Every link and every name, and why the two disagree

Top: the 1,784 links (A links to B). Bottom: the 1,869 pairs where A's prose names B. Blue is the 1,454 pairs that are both. Click a segment to list its pairs, and click a pair to read the sentence on A's page and the line of A's wiki source where the link sits (or doesn't).

A link with no name (330 links). The largest group, 122 links (37%), is the Hawkeye problem from the other side. The text says Ghost Rider, Spider-Woman, Psylocke or Giant-Man, and the editor linked the specific holder: Johnny Blaze, Jessica Drew, Betsy Braddock, Hank Pym. Our rule either sends the codename to the name page or, when several characters claim it and Wikipedia won't choose, to nobody. Another 81 links sit on words that aren't on our name lists, such as an alter ego (Chris Powell for Darkhawk, Wendell Vaughn for Quasar) or a description. Seventy-four are not in the prose at all: they're in a reference, an italic comic title or the infobox. Thirty-two are only in In other media, which we left out. Only 21 are names our matcher should have caught and didn't.

A name with no link (415 pairs). In 165 of them the editor did link those words, but to a different page. Forty-seven links go to another of our 303 pages (the text says Human Torch, the link says the android). One hundred eighteen go to a page outside the corpus: Clint Barton in 29 pairs, Carol Danvers in 16 (as Captain Marvel and Ms. Marvel), Dane Whitman, Kamala Khan, Mar-Vell, Ben Reilly. Some of those 118 go to films, storylines and a novel, so a few are our own title errors. In the other 250 pairs, the words are never linked on the page at all.

05 · Reading the textThe names nobody linked

The 250 names nobody linked are where our measurement and Wikipedia disagree most directly, so we read them. We took a random 40 and checked each sentence. 25 are real: the page names the character and doesn't link them. For example, Ulysses Bloodstone's page says "Elsa Bloodstone is the daughter of Ulysses Bloodstone", and that sentence has no link. Hercules forms the Mighty Avengers with Hank Pym, also without a link. 15 are our errors: 6 titles (Wolverine and the X-Men, World War Hulk, Moon Girl and Devil Dinosaur), 4 things named after a character (the Infinity Gauntlet, the Blade, which is a sword), 3 other characters with the same name (a Kree Sentry robot, Black Tarantula) and 2 codenames sent to the page about the name instead of the holder's own page.

Our matcher is right 90% of the time overall, but only 62.5% of the time on names nobody linked.

That's the most useful thing we learned about measuring with text. Our errors aren't spread evenly. A wrong match is almost never linked, so the errors pile up in exactly the pairs where text and links disagree. Those are the pairs you'd write a post about. "The text finds 250 relations the links miss" would have been more than a third wrong. You only find that out by reading them.

06 · Co-mentionsWho is named in the same sentence?

The mention network asks "does A's page name B?". The co-mention network asks "are A and B named together?", on any page. It's the network the course's entity-extraction exercise builds, and it has a choice that a link network doesn't have: the unit. Two names in one sentence is strong evidence. Two names on one page is weak. We built it at three units and several thresholds, and scored each version against the (undirected) links:

Figure 3 · The unit is a dial

Co-mention networks scored against the links

Roll calls: share of co-mentioned pairs that are linked, by how many characters the sentence names

Top: each point is one co-mention network, with an edge when two characters are named together in at least k sentences, paragraphs or pages (k = 1…6, labelled). Across: the share of the 1,434 linked pairs it finds (recall). Up: the share of its edges that are linked (precision). The dashed line is chance: 3.1% of all pairs are linked. Bottom: every pair from every sentence, grouped by the number of characters that sentence names.

At the sentence level, the co-mention network finds 68% of the linked pairs, and two thirds of its edges are links. Requiring three shared sentences raises precision to 86% but finds only a quarter of the links. Page-level co-mention finds 91% of the links at 23% precision, which is mostly noise. The bottom chart shows where the noise comes from: roll calls. When a sentence names two characters, 86% of the pairs are linked. When it lists six or more ("…Doctor Doom, Man-Thing, Luke Cage, Moon Girl, Devil Dinosaur…") it's 45%. One sentence like that adds 15 or more edges. Pairs named together on someone else's page are linked 63% of the time, against 87% when one of the two is the page's own subject.

07 · What surprised usThe network was never about names

We expected the disagreements to be about coverage: names in the text that nobody bothered to link. There are some (section 5), but most disagreements are about identity. A name like Hawkeye is shared, and a link has to say who. Wikipedia's category puts the shared-name page in our corpus and leaves the people out, so the link network quietly loses Clint Barton, Carol Danvers and Kamala Khan, three of Marvel's best-known heroes. The text network keeps them, merged into their codenames. Neither is the "real" Marvel network. The links are precise about people and blind to anyone outside the category. The text sees everyone but can't tell two Hawkeyes apart.

The other surprise was how exact the course data is: the week-1 edge list is the [[links]] in the page source, with 1,781 of 1,784 edges reproduced. So every disagreement could be explained from the source rather than guessed.

09 · LimitationsWhat this can't tell you

  • Names only, no pronouns. "She" and "he" never count, so a sentence about Storm that says "she" adds nothing. The co-mention network only sees explicit names.
  • Our name lists are incomplete. 80 links sit on alter egos and nicknames that neither Wikidata nor the first sentence gave us (Chris Powell, Wendell Vaughn).
  • Shared codenames are unresolvable from the name alone. We send them to the name page or to nobody. Telling Clint Barton from Kate Bishop would need the surrounding story, which a contextual model might do. We didn't try.
  • 90% precision is an estimate from 80 matches (roughly ±7 points), and it is lower among the pairs that disagree (62.5% from 40). Treat any single "named but not linked" pair as unverified until you read it, which Figures 1 and 2 let you do.
  • Revision drift. The wiki source is the last revision on or before the snapshot date, and three course edges don't appear in it. Twelve pages were merged or renamed after the snapshot (ten members of Strikeforce: Morituri, Dargo Ktor and Vindicator), so we fetched each one's own history.
